Summer Quest

I wasn't trying to build an app. I was trying to solve a very mundane human problem: getting people off their couches and out into an 80-degree New York summer. Together.

It all started with a party.

To me, birthdays aren't really about being celebrated, but an excuse to celebrate life with the people I love. So instead of organizing one party, I designed and launched NYC Summer Quest, a month-long social game that turned New York City into a shared playground for two different friend groups.

The challenge was designing one shared experience that could connect people with different lifestyles, friend circles, and levels of commitment.

The app was the delivery mechanism.
The experience was the product.

01

The problem

The celebration outgrew the party.

I don't really do birthday parties. So when my friend Erika suggested a joint celebration, I wanted to make it memorable, bigger-than-life, and more than a one-off gathering.

The first idea was a month-long bingo game. It got both of us excited, but it had a problem: people could play to win without actually exploring, and a static card gave them no feedback, no sense of progress, and nothing connecting one adventure to the next.

The bingo gradually turned into a scavenger hunt, and then into something extra ambitious. I knew scavenger-hunt apps already existed, but that wasn't really the point. I was trying to make this summer feel a little more special for my friends, and there was a difference between “download this app and play with me” and “I made this for you. Go live your best summer.”

Instead of asking for gifts, I made one.

So I turned the static idea into a mobile-first web app with its own visual world, rules, progression, and memory of what happened along the way. Building it myself also meant I could take the idea all the way from product strategy to a working launch instead of stopping at a prototype.

Summer Quest started with a hunch: What if “we should hang out sometime” came with something specific to actually do? I wanted to see whether a lightweight game could make it easier to turn vague intentions into actual plans, without requiring everyone to coordinate schedules or participate in exactly the same way.

Starting assumptions

For the first edition, the players were my New Yorker friends in their 20s–40s, with very different levels of familiarity with the city, from long-time residents to newcomers. Within that group, Summer Quest would test three bets:

  1. People could participate more easily without coordinating schedules or progressing together.
  2. Quests could create enough momentum to turn vague intentions into concrete activities.
  3. Separate adventures could still feel connected without everyone following the same path.

That left me with the question shaping the rest of the product:

How do you design a shared experience when everyone participates differently?

02

The principles

One shared experience. Many ways to live it.

The hard part wasn't coming up with 25 things to do. It was designing a game that could survive people's actual lives.

My friends lived in different neighborhoods, kept different schedules, had different budgets, and had wildly different appetites for commitment. Some would want to make a whole summer out of it; others might join for an afternoon. If the game only worked for people with plenty of time, money, friends, or commitment, I would have rebuilt the exact barriers I was trying to escape.

So I designed the system around five principles.

Principle A

Shared didn't mean synchronized.

Being part of the same game didn't require following the same path. People could choose any quest, at any time, and a quick weekend dabble or a summer-long run were both valid ways to participate.

Tradeoff

Less control over how the experience unfolded.

The game loop was not the value loop.

The game mechanics created momentum: choose a quest, complete it, earn points, see progress, then pick the next one. But the mechanics were never the value on their own. The part I actually cared about happened mostly after the phone went away: a prompt became an invitation, the invitation became an experience, and the experience became something worth remembering.

03

The decisions

The important action was never tapping “Submit.”

It was going somewhere, trying something new, or inviting someone along.

So I kept every design decision focused on one question: does this get people into the experience, or does it keep them in the app?

Gameplay

Real life set the pace.

Twenty-four quests were open from the start, with no prescribed route or pace. People could choose what fit that day, bring whoever happened to be around, or fold several quests into an outing already happening. The finale was the one exception: it stayed locked behind a secret code and was saved for the birthday gathering as a shared moment of discovery.

The Adventure Board became home base, giving people enough structure to see what was possible and choose what came next without dictating the path.

I gave up control over the path so the game could fit around real life.

Behavioral design

The game is not a popularity contest.

My first scoring system was intentionally simple: every quest was worth the same. But equal points did not reflect equal effort, and an uncapped social bonus could easily reward having the largest friend group more than actually doing interesting things.

So I weighted quest values based on effort, capped friend bonuses at five people, and introduced identity-based ranks for a New York pride boost.

Experience architecture

Separate planning from remembering.

My first idea was to turn completed quest tiles into participants' own photos. It made progress visible, but it also made the Adventure Board noisier at exactly the moment it needed to answer one question quickly: What should I do next?

So I split the jobs. The Adventure Board stayed lightweight and forward-looking, while completing a quest unlocked a short reflection and added the experience to the Summer Journal. Photos and captions could accumulate there into a personal record of the summer, and the Photo Keepsake turned that story into something people could save or print afterward.

Product strategy

Build less. Leave room for more.

Before building, I mapped possible features against two questions: How much value does this add to the experience, and how much complexity does it introduce? That helped separate what I needed to test from what merely sounded useful.

Accounts, messaging, GPS verification, cloud sync, and a live leaderboard could all make the product more sophisticated, but none were necessary to test the core experience. I focused on letting people choose their own path and collect their memories, and eventually added basic analytics to see how they played.

I also kept the game structure separate from the birthday-specific content so quests, themes, rewards, and shared moments could change without rebuilding the product.

04

The Reality Check

How a birthday game became a live behavioral study

The game worked, but not exactly how I expected.

I designed Summer Quest so people could share one experience without having to participate the same way. Then I watched them interpret that freedom thirteen different ways.

Some played mostly solo. Others regularly brought friends. Some completed only a few quests; others nearly filled the board. No two people followed the same path, and 79.5% of completions had already happened before August 16, which meant the game was already unfolding across different neighborhoods, schedules, and social circles long before everyone ended up in the same park.

Shared really did not have to mean synchronized.

5 representative player journeys

= friends

Social started with an invitation, not a requirement.

About 65% of recorded quests involved friends, even though almost every quest could be completed alone.

The two deliberately group-based quests told a different story. Human Pyramid was completed only once, by three players. The finale was designed to happen at the gathering, and most, but not all, of the people there attempted it. I wasn’t disappointed. If anything, my friends were more invested in actual conversations around the birthday cake than opening the app, which was exactly the goal.

The honor system became an interpretation system.

I deliberately kept completion lightweight. No GPS checks, no approval queue, no proof-heavy verification. For a game between friends, policing participation felt much worse than the possibility that someone might bend the rules.

And bend them they did.

A few players matched quests to things they had done long before the game started. The timeframe was in the rules, but I’d forgotten to enforce it in the date picker. A small technical fumble on my part - live and learn.

That did not make me regret the honor system, but it changed what I could actually claim from the data. A completion told me that someone considered an experience part of Summer Quest. It did not necessarily mean Summer Quest caused that experience to happen.

The Journal did not require everyone to become a novelist.

I wanted people to caption their adventures and make the summer story their own. I also assumed most people probably would not sit there composing tiny memoirs after every quest.

Both turned out to be true.

42% of completed quests included a participant-written caption. Some people added almost nothing beyond the built-in reflection. Others used short captions as little memory cues. A few treated the Journal much more like a diary.

The same feature ended up serving players in different ways: an automatically assembled archive for some, a lightly personalized scrapbook or even a diary for others.

Play didn't stop at the finale party.

August 16 created the biggest moment of overlap. People compared progress, exchanged keepsakes, did quests together, and celebrated around the picnic table.

Then some of them kept playing. One friend started at the picnic, completed three quests that day, and finished the rest of her board over the following two weeks. Others returned afterward too.

05

The Lessons

One game. A reusable system.

Shipping Summer Quest made the tradeoffs real. Flexibility helped people participate on their own terms, but the honor system also showed me where freedom needed clearer boundaries. Some players wanted more visibility into what everyone else was doing, while the Journal became useful in more ways than I originally expected.

I had already separated the reusable game structure from the birthday-specific content, which meant the quests, theme, rewards, visual world, and shared moments could all change without rebuilding the experience from scratch. A bachelorette party, a corporate bonding activity, or winter travel to another continent could use the same underlying participation model while feeling completely different.

Several survey respondents said they would play another version, and a few people came to me directly asking for more. A second experiment would give me a better chance to test where added structure actually earns its place: more social visibility without more pressure, clearer completion boundaries without heavy verification, and deeper personalization without asking people to do more work.

I am not writing a business proposal for my passion project just yet.

And, perhaps more importantly, it delivered my most social birthday yet.
