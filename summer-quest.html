Summer Quest

I wasn't trying to build an app. I was trying to solve a harder problem: getting people off their couches and out into an 80-degree New York summer. Together.

It all started with a party.

To me, birthdays aren't really about being celebrated but an excuse to celebrate life with the people I love. So instead of organizing one gathering, I designed and launched NYC Summer Quest: a month-long social game that turned New York City into a shared playground for two different friend groups.

The app was the delivery mechanism.
The experience was the product.

01

The problem

The celebration outgrew the party.

I don't really do birthday parties. So when my friend Erika suggested a joint celebration, I wanted to make it memorable, bigger-than-life, and more than a one-off gathering.

Go big or go home, right?

The first idea was a month-long bingo game. It got both of us excited, but it had a problem: people could play to win without actually exploring. A static card also gave them no feedback, no sense of progress, and nothing connecting one adventure to the next.

The bingo morphed into a scavenger hunt. I knew scavenger-hunt apps already existed. That wasn't really the point.

I was trying to make this summer feel a little more special for my friends. There was a difference between “download this app and play with me” and “I made this for you. Go live your best summer.”

Instead of asking for gifts, I made one.

So I turned the static image into a mobile-first web app with its own visual world, rules, progression, and memory of what happened along the way.

Building it myself gave me another challenge I wanted: learn to vibe code and see whether I could take an idea all the way from product strategy to a working launch instead of stopping at a prototype.

Once I started designing the game, the birthday problem became something bigger.

Summer Quest started with a hunch: What if “we should hang out sometime” came with something specific to actually do?

Starting assumptions

For the first edition, the players were New Yorkers in their 20s–40s, with very different levels of familiarity with the city, from New York natives to bright-eyed newcomers.

Within that group, Summer Quest would test three bets:

  1. People could participate more easily without coordinating schedules or progressing together.
  2. Quests could create enough momentum to turn vague intentions into concrete activities.
  3. Separate adventures could still feel connected without everyone following the same path.

That left me with the question shaping the rest of the product:

How do you design a shared experience when everyone participates differently?

02

The principles

One shared experience. Many ways to live it.

The hard part wasn't coming up with 25 things to do. It was designing a game that could survive people's actual lives.

My friends lived in different neighborhoods, kept different schedules, and had wildly different appetites for commitment. If everyone had to play the same way, at the same pace, with the same people, I would have rebuilt the exact coordination problem I was trying to escape.

So five principles shaped the system.

Principle A

Shared didn't mean synchronized.

Being part of the same game didn't require following the same path. People could choose any quest, at any time, and a quick weekend dabble or a summer-long run were both valid ways to play.

Use the arrows to move through the 5 principles.

Tradeoff

Less control over how the game unfolded.

The game loop was not the value loop.

The game mechanics created momentum: choose a quest, complete it, earn points, see progress, then pick the next one. But the mechanics were never the value on their own. The part I actually cared about happened mostly after the phone went away: a prompt became an invitation, the invitation became an experience, and the experience became something worth remembering.

The game loop kept people moving. The value loop made moving worthwhile.

03

The design

The important action was never tapping "Submit."

It was going somewhere, trying something new, or inviting someone along.

So I kept every design decision focused on one question: does this get people into the experience, or does it keep them in the app?

Gameplay

Real life set the pace, not the game.

24 of the 25 quests were open from the start, with no prescribed route or pace. People could choose what fit that day, bring whoever happened to be around, and knock out several quests in one outing if the stars, subway schedule, and group chat happened to align. The finale quest was the exception: it stayed locked behind a secret code and was saved for the birthday gathering as one designed moment of discovery.

The Adventure Board became home base: enough structure to see what was possible, choose something, and move on. Completion worked on an honor system rather than GPS checks or proof-heavy verification. For a casual game between friends, the friction of policing participation mattered more than the risk of someone bending the rules.

I gave up control over the path so the game could fit around real life instead of dictating it.

Behavioral design

Reward the behavior, not the popularity contest.

My first scoring system was intentionally simple: every quest was worth the same. But equal points didn't reflect equal effort, and an uncapped social bonus could easily reward having the largest friend group more than actually doing interesting things.

So the system evolved into weighted quest values, bounded bonuses, and identity-based ranks. Harder adventures earned more; bringing friends added points, but only up to a cap; and progress moved through ranks instead of a public leaderboard.

Social experience

Invitation, not requirement.

I wanted Summer Quest to make it easier to do things together, but forcing people into teams would have recreated the scheduling problem I was trying to escape.

A quest could happen alone, with one friend, or with five different people who had nothing to do with the last quest. Friend bonuses added a small nudge toward inviting someone without turning another person's availability into a blocker. I wasn't trying to build another place to socialize online. I was trying to create more reasons to end up in the same place as someone else.

Experience architecture

Separate planning from remembering.

My first idea was to turn completed quest tiles into participants' own photos. It made progress visible, but it also made the Adventure Board noisier at exactly the moment it needed to answer one question quickly: What should I do next?

So I split the jobs. The Adventure Board stayed lightweight and forward-looking. Completing a quest unlocked a short reflection as a small discovery moment, while the Summer Journal gave photos and captions somewhere to accumulate into a record of the summer. The Photo Keepsake then turned that story into something people could save or print afterward.

Product strategy

Build less. Leave room for more.

Before building, I mapped possible features against two questions: How much value does this add to the experience, and how much complexity does it introduce? That helped separate what I needed to test from what merely sounded useful.

I also kept the game structure separate from the birthday-specific content so quests, themes, rewards, and shared moments could change later without rebuilding everything.

04

The Reality Check

How a birthday game became a live behavioral study

The game worked, but not exactly how I expected.

I designed Summer Quest so people could share one experience without having to participate the same way. Then I watched them interpret that freedom thirteen different ways. Some played mostly solo. Others regularly brought friends. Some completed only a few quests, others nearly filled the board.

Shared did not have to mean synchronized.

79.5% of Phase 1 completions happened before August 16. By the time the birthday arrived, the game had already been unfolding across different neighborhoods, schedules, and social circles for weeks.

Social worked better as an invitation than a requirement.

About 65% of recorded quests involved friends, even though almost every quest could be completed solo.

I included two deliberately group-based quests to test the opposite model. Human Pyramid was completed only once, by three players. The finale quest was designed to happen at the birthday party, and most, but not all, of the players who were there attempted it.

Trust kept the game light. It also stretched the rules.

Most people played within the spirit of the honor system, but a few interpreted that freedom very far outside the box. At least two players matched quests to experiences that happened before launch date, while others submitted completions that barely matched the original prompt.

The principle still held: trust was the right default. But flexibility needed clearer boundaries around what counted.

The memory layer worked without turning everyone into a novelist.

I wanted people to caption their adventures and make the story fully their own. I also designed for the likelihood that most people wouldn't.

42% of completed quests included a participant-written caption. That made the prewritten reflections important: every completion still added something to the summer story.

Some players barely added anything. Others made the Journal distinctly their own.

People kept playing after the birthday.

August 16 was designed as a midpoint milestone and an opportunity to do quests together. The group chat lit up, photo keepsakes were exchanged, and prizes were given around the picnic table.

Afterward, a handful of players kept playing over the following weeks. One friend came to the picnic, got excited seeing everyone's photos, and started playing that day. She did three quests there, finished the rest of her board afterward, and logged them all at once two weeks later.

The sample was small, but the birthday did not function as a finish line.

05

The Lessons

One game. A reusable system.

Shipping Summer Quest made the tradeoffs real. Some players wanted more visibility into what others were doing, while the honor system showed me that creativity is great for adventures, less so for interpreting the rules.

I already knew scavenger-hunt apps existed and shared many of the same mechanics. What interested me was something more specific: highly tailored experiences built around a particular group, place, occasion, or story. The quests, theme, rewards, visual world, and memory content could all change while the core participation model stayed intact. Same skeleton, completely different experience.

Several survey respondents expressed interest in future versions, and a few people came to me directly asking for more. A second experiment would test where more structure actually earns its place: more social visibility without more pressure, clearer completion boundaries without heavy verification, and deeper personalization without asking players to do more work.

I am not writing a business proposal for my passion project just yet. But Summer Quest proved the bespoke concept is worth testing beyond a birthday.

And, perhaps more importantly, it delivered my most social birthday yet.
