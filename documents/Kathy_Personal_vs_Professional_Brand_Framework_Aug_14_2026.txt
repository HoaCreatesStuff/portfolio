KATHY — PERSONAL BRAND VS. PROFESSIONAL BRAND FRAMEWORK

1. THE CORE DISTINCTION

Your professional brand answers:

“Why should someone hire me as a Product Designer?”

Your personal brand answers:

“Why will they remember me instead of the other 30 qualified designers they saw this week?”

They are not competing identities.

The goal is:

Professional credibility first. Personal distinctiveness throughout.

Your personality should make the work more memorable, not make the work harder to understand.


2. PROFESSIONAL BRAND

Core positioning

Mid-level Product Designer who combines strategic product thinking, strong visual craft, behavioral insight, and hands-on execution to turn ambiguous ideas into thoughtful, usable experiences.

You should come across as someone who:

- understands the product beyond the interface
- asks why something should exist before deciding how it should look
- makes deliberate tradeoffs
- thinks about behavior, motivation, systems, and real-world constraints
- can move from concept through implementation
- works comfortably across UX, UI, product strategy, prototyping, testing, and iteration
- has unusually strong visual storytelling ability for a product designer
- uses AI and technical tools as implementation collaborators without confusing tool usage with design ownership


PROFESSIONAL ATTRIBUTES TO COMMUNICATE

1. Strategic

You connect design decisions to:

- user behavior
- product goals
- business or experience outcomes
- constraints
- risks
- downstream consequences

You do not present design as a sequence of screens.

You present it as a sequence of decisions.


2. Product-minded

You naturally ask questions such as:

- What actually needs to be built?
- What can be simplified?
- Which behavior are we trying to encourage?
- What does the user need at this moment?
- Where does complexity add value?
- Where should the product deliberately do less?
- What happens after launch?

This should separate you from candidates who mainly demonstrate UX process compliance.


3. Behaviorally aware

You are interested in how products influence:

- motivation
- participation
- habits
- social behavior
- emotional response
- perceived effort
- trust
- reward

Summer Quest is particularly strong evidence of this.


4. Strong visual craft

Visual design is a major strength, but it should be framed as a product capability, not your entire identity.

Your work should demonstrate:

- hierarchy
- information design
- interaction clarity
- visual systems
- editorial composition
- emotional tone
- polished execution

The message is not:

“I make things beautiful.”

It is:

“I use visual design deliberately to shape comprehension, behavior, and experience.”


5. Builder mentality

You are comfortable getting close to implementation.

Evidence may include:

- working prototypes
- live products
- AI-assisted development
- implementation decisions
- debugging
- responsive behavior
- technical tradeoffs
- analytics
- iteration after launch

This positions you closer to a modern product designer than a traditional handoff-only UX designer.


6. Evidence-driven, but not process-theater-driven

You should show evidence when it matters:

- analytics
- usability observations
- survey results
- interviews
- behavioral signals
- iteration history

But you do not need to manufacture a textbook research process for projects that did not require one.

Your portfolio should communicate:

“I use the amount and type of research appropriate to the decision.”

That is more credible than pretending every project began with twelve personas and an affinity map summoned from the UX underworld.


3. PERSONAL BRAND

Core identity

Your personal brand should feel:

Warm. Expressive. Curious. Observant. Playful. Editorial. Slightly unexpected.

Not “quirky” as a performance.

Not “fun designer™.”

The personality comes from how you see and describe the world.


PERSONAL QUALITIES TO PRESERVE

1. Curiosity

You notice strange details.

You chase questions.

You enjoy understanding why something behaves the way it does.

That curiosity can appear through:

- project origins
- side projects
- observations
- research questions
- unexpected product hypotheses


2. Storytelling instinct

You naturally reconstruct:

- context
- emotion
- environment
- tension
- sequence
- surprise

Your strongest case studies should therefore feel like stories with:

1. setup
2. tension
3. decisions
4. consequences
5. resolution

Not:

Research → Wireframes → Testing → Final UI → Thank you for scrolling 6,000 pixels.


3. Expressiveness

You are comfortable allowing enthusiasm, frustration, surprise, humor, and affection for the work to show.

The writing can occasionally sound like a human being actually experienced the project.

This is an advantage.

Protect it.


4. Pattern recognition

One of your strongest natural tendencies is making unusual comparisons that reveal something useful.

Your analogies work best when they:

- clarify complexity
- expose a pattern
- make an abstract idea concrete
- create a memorable hook

The analogy should support the reasoning.

It should never replace it.


5. Editorial sensibility

Your personal visual identity should feel more like:

- a designed publication
- a curious object
- an editorial story
- an expressive digital space

than:

- generic SaaS portfolio template
- brutalist designer clone
- pastel productivity app
- hyper-minimal Swiss résumé with one enormous Helvetica sentence declaring that you “craft experiences”

Human civilization has produced enough of those.


6. Warmth without sentimentality

Your work frequently deals with:

- people
- memory
- connection
- motivation
- everyday life
- shared experiences

That emotional intelligence should remain visible.

But the portfolio should not become overly earnest.

The warmth should come from the work itself.


4. THE INTERSECTION

This is where your strongest brand actually lives.

You are not:

Strategist OR visual designer

You are:

A visual storyteller who thinks in product systems and behavioral decisions.

You are not:

Creative OR analytical

You are:

Creative in how you frame problems and analytical in how you decide what to do about them.

You are not:

Professional OR personal

You are:

Professionally rigorous, personally recognizable.


5. BRAND ARCHITECTURE

Think of the portfolio as three layers.

Layer 1 — Immediate professional signal

A recruiter should understand within seconds:

Kathy is a Product Designer.

They should quickly see evidence of:

- product thinking
- shipped work
- strategic decisions
- UX/UI ability
- visual quality

No mystery box.

Do not make recruiters solve an escape room to determine your job title.


Layer 2 — Differentiation

After several minutes, the impression should become:

“She approaches products differently.”

They notice:

- behavioral reasoning
- unusually thoughtful framing
- strong visual storytelling
- product judgment
- systems thinking
- willingness to build


Layer 3 — Personal recognition

After reading multiple projects:

“I know what Kathy feels like as a designer.”

They recognize:

- your voice
- curiosity
- humor
- visual atmosphere
- way of noticing things
- preference for meaningful and slightly unusual problems

That is personal branding working correctly.


6. PORTFOLIO VOICE BALANCE

The portfolio writing should roughly prioritize:

30% Strategic reasoning
Why decisions were made.

25% Storytelling and context
What was happening and why it mattered.

20% Product / UX explanation
How the experience works.

15% Evidence
Results, testing, observations, metrics.

5% Personal reflection
What surprised you or changed your thinking.

5% Wit / analogy / quirk
Small moments that make the writing unmistakably yours.

The last 5% should mostly come naturally from you rather than being manufactured.


7. WHAT PROFESSIONAL BRANDING CONTROLS

Professional branding should determine:

- project selection
- project hierarchy
- case-study structure
- titles and role descriptions
- decision framing
- metrics
- process explanation
- capabilities shown
- résumé language
- navigation clarity
- credibility signals

It establishes competence.


8. WHAT PERSONAL BRANDING CONTROLS

Personal branding can influence:

- visual atmosphere
- typography
- transitions
- imagery
- project hooks
- captions
- writing rhythm
- analogy
- microcopy
- personal introduction
- About page
- project selection
- small unexpected interactions

It creates recognition.


9. WHAT NOT TO TURN INTO “BRAND”

You do not need:

- a personal logo
- a forced catchphrase
- a signature emoji
- a branded adjective stack
- an invented design philosophy
- a “creative rebel” persona
- constant jokes
- every project rendered in identical colors
- a personal manifesto on every page

Brand is the pattern people infer from repeated evidence.

It is not something you have to announce every thirteen seconds.


10. PORTFOLIO VISUAL DIRECTION

LOCKED DIRECTION

Atmospheric Editorial × Graphic Art Direction

The portfolio should not visually choose between:
- professional polish
and
- dramatic personality

Its identity comes from controlling both.

Core principle:

Soft atmosphere. Hard hierarchy.

The atmosphere may be:
- dreamy
- hazy
- colorful
- warm
- slightly surreal

The hierarchy should be:
- decisive
- graphic
- editorial
- high-contrast
- compositionally confident

This prevents the atmospheric pink / lilac / blue world from becoming overly youthful or decorative.

The visual identity should feel closer to an art-directed independent publication than a conventional UX portfolio template.


PROFESSIONAL CLARITY STILL CONTROLS

- navigation
- body-text readability
- case-study architecture
- evidence hierarchy
- captions
- metadata
- interaction predictability
- accessibility
- responsive behavior

PERSONAL / ART-DIRECTION EXPRESSION CAN CONTROL

- homepage atmosphere
- chapter-opening compositions
- large typographic statements
- asymmetry
- image cropping
- photography
- project-specific visual worlds
- color fields
- visual rhythm
- selected motion
- occasional handwritten accents
- transitions between quiet and dramatic sections


PRINT-EDITORIAL INFLUENCE

Use print-magazine principles as compositional grammar, not as literal imitation.

Translate:
- spread-like chapter openings
- dramatic type scale
- small metadata against large statements
- asymmetrical image / text relationships
- side notes
- pull statements
- thin rules / chapter markers with actual meaning
- aggressive but intentional cropping
- whitespace as part of the composition

Do not import:
- tiny print body text
- long multi-column reading
- fake page numbers / crop marks / barcodes
- fixed desktop spreads
- page-turn gimmicks

Desktop may feel like a spread.

Mobile should become a strong vertical composition.


TWO-STAGE VISUAL AUDIENCE

Round 1 — Recruiter / general hiring screen

The work must be immediately legible:
- Product Designer
- strong projects
- clear role
- clear outcomes / status
- easy navigation

Round 2 — Design manager / design lead

The work must additionally demonstrate:
- art direction
- typography
- composition
- image selection and crop
- grid control
- rhythm
- restraint
- motion judgment
- visual concept
- project-specific systems
- responsive translation
- originality

Recruiter clarity is the floor.

Visual craft is part of the proof.

A merely polished portfolio would undersell one of Kathy's strongest differentiators.

VISUAL QUALITY TEST

Professional:
Does this make me easier to hire?

Personal:
Does this feel recognizably mine?

Art direction:
Could another competent Product Designer have designed this exact page?

If the answer to the third question is yes too often, the portfolio may still be visually generic.

Personality should not sit only in the homepage hero.

The art direction must survive into the case studies.



11. PERSONAL PROJECTS AS BRAND EVIDENCE

Your personal projects should help reveal your worldview.

Good topics for you naturally involve:

- behavior
- curiosity
- everyday rituals
- social dynamics
- memory
- perception
- culture
- decision-making
- overlooked ordinary experiences

This is more valuable than producing random fake SaaS dashboards just because hiring managers supposedly enjoy dashboards.

A strong set of projects should collectively answer:

“What kinds of things does Kathy notice that another designer might not?”


12. CASE-STUDY TEST

Every major case study should prove at least three dimensions.

Product competence
Can she make sound product decisions?

Craft
Can she execute the experience well?

Perspective
Is there something distinctive about how she approaches the problem?

The strongest projects prove all three.


13. BRAND CONSISTENCY TEST

Before publishing anything, ask:

Professional

- Does this make me look like a stronger Product Designer?
- Is my contribution obvious?
- Can someone identify the important decisions?
- Is there evidence behind my claims?
- Am I demonstrating judgment rather than merely process?

Personal

- Does this feel like something I would actually make?
- Is there a point of view?
- Does any personality survive?
- Is there something memorable here?
- Have I accidentally polished away everything human?

Combined

- Is the personality helping the story rather than competing with it?
- Does the work remain easy to evaluate?
- Would someone remember both the project and me?


14. DESIRED RECRUITER TAKEAWAY

After scanning:

“Strong Product Designer. Excellent visual craft.”

After reading one case study:

“She thinks carefully about behavior, product decisions, and tradeoffs.”

After reading several:

“She has a recognizable point of view.”

From a design manager / design lead:

“She has real art-direction judgment, not just polished UI.”

After an interview:

“She is strategic, curious, articulate, visually strong, and capable of taking substantial ownership.”

That progression is the brand.


15. SHORT INTERNAL BRAND STATEMENT

This does not have to appear publicly.

Use it as a filter for portfolio decisions:

“I’m a Product Designer who turns messy ideas and human behavior into thoughtful, visually compelling experiences. I combine product strategy, behavioral reasoning, storytelling, and strong craft, while bringing curiosity and personality to the way I frame problems.”


16. SIMPLEST VERSION

When deciding whether something belongs in the portfolio:

Professional brand asks:
Does this prove I am good at the job I want?

Personal brand asks:
Does this feel recognizably mine?

The portfolio succeeds when the answer to both is yes.
